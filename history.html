Chapter One

Where the Journey Began

People have crossed and climbed mountains for thousands of years. Pilgrims sought sacred summits, herders and traders followed high passes, hunters and surveyors pushed into the heights, and naturalists climbed to study glaciers, plants and weather.

Ancient mountain travel was mostly a means to an end. Modern mountaineering is different: the climb itself, with its planning, skill and risk, becomes the purpose. That shift happened gradually, mainly in the Alps during the 18th and 19th centuries, as scientific curiosity and a growing taste for adventure came together.

There is no single date on which recreational climbing began. Earlier ascents, such as Mont Aiguille in 1492, show that people climbed for their own reasons long before clubs and guidebooks existed.

Timeline

Milestones That Changed Mountaineering

  1. 1786

    First Recorded Ascent of Mont Blanc

    Michel-Gabriel Paccard and Jacques Balmat reached the summit of Mont Blanc, a key milestone in the rise of modern European mountaineering.

  2. 1854

    Wetterhorn Ascent

    Alfred Wills' ascent of the Wetterhorn helped popularize climbing in Britain and is linked with the start of the Victorian mountaineering era.

  3. 1857

    The Alpine Club

    Founded in London, the Alpine Club is widely regarded as the world's first mountaineering club, helping establish climbing as an organized activity.

  4. 1865

    The Matterhorn

    Edward Whymper's party reached the summit. Four members died on the descent, a tragedy that became a defining moment in climbing history.

  5. 1953

    Mount Everest

    Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay made the first confirmed ascent of Everest, as part of a large British expedition.

  6. 1978

    Everest Without Supplemental Oxygen

    Reinhold Messner and Peter Habeler made the first confirmed ascent of Everest without supplemental oxygen.

  7. 1980

    Winter Everest Ascent

    Leszek Cichy and Krzysztof Wielicki completed the first successful winter ascent of Everest.

  8. Today

    The Modern Era

    Better forecasting, equipment, communications, expedition medicine and environmental awareness continue to shape how people climb.

1850s – 1860s

The Golden Age of Alpinism

The mid-19th century is generally called the Golden Age of Alpinism. In little over a decade, climbers made first ascents of many of the Alps' great summits, including the Wetterhorn, the Eiger's neighbours and the Matterhorn.

  • Organized climbing: guided parties and planned routes replaced improvised attempts.
  • Clubs: the Alpine Club (1857) was followed by clubs across Europe.
  • Literature: climbing books and journals spread the appeal of the mountains.
  • Local guides: Alpine guides were central to many first ascents.

Chapter Three

Beyond the Alps: The Himalayas and Karakoram

Early Exploration

Surveys and exploratory journeys in the 19th and early 20th centuries mapped the great ranges long before serious climbing attempts.

Everest in the 20th Century

Reconnaissance and attempts through the 1920s–1950s built the knowledge that made the 1953 success possible. Hillary and Tenzing's ascent was a team achievement, supported by many climbers and Sherpa colleagues.

Local Knowledge & High-Altitude Porters

Sherpa, Balti, Hunza and other mountain communities carried loads, fixed routes and shared deep knowledge of terrain and weather. Their skill and risk were fundamental to Himalayan mountaineering, and many were climbers in their own right.

The Karakoram

Home to K2 (first climbed in 1954 by an Italian expedition) and other 8,000-metre peaks, the Karakoram is known for harsh weather, technical terrain and remote approaches.

Modern Expedition Planning

Logistics, acclimatization schedules, weather windows and medical planning now shape high-altitude expeditions.

Equipment

From Traditional Gear to Modern Technology

Early Boots & Clothing

Nailed leather boots, wool and canvas gave limited warmth and weather protection.

Ropes, Pitons & Carabiners

Natural-fibre ropes gave way to nylon, while pitons and carabiners made protected climbing possible.

Ice Axes & Crampons

Axes and crampons evolved steadily, enabling steeper snow and ice terrain.

Helmets & Technical Protection

Helmets, harnesses and removable protection improved safety on rock and ice.

Tents & Expedition Clothing

Lightweight fabrics, insulation and sturdy tents made higher camps more survivable.

GPS, Satellite & Forecasting

Navigation devices, satellite communication and detailed forecasts support decisions on the mountain.

Skills

The Evolution of Climbing Techniques

01

Alpine Routes

Traditional routes combining glacier travel, snow slopes and rock ridges.

02

Rock Climbing

Technical protection and movement skills developed into a discipline of its own.

03

Ice & Mixed

Better tools and technique opened frozen waterfalls and mixed rock-and-ice faces.

04

Expedition Strategy

Staged camps, load carrying and careful timing for high-altitude objectives.

05

Altitude Medicine

Gradual acclimatization and medical knowledge help reduce altitude-related risk.

06

Safety & Stewardship

Risk planning and "leave no trace" practices are now central to responsible climbing.

Techniques vary with terrain, weather, route difficulty and the experience of the team.

Pioneers

Women Who Expanded the World of Climbing

Lucy Walker

Victorian pioneer

A leading woman climber of the 1860s–1870s, Walker made many Alpine ascents and in 1871 became the first woman to climb the Matterhorn.

Junko Tabei

Everest, 1975

In 1975 Tabei of Japan became the first woman to reach the summit of Mount Everest, and later completed the Seven Summits.

Wanda Rutkiewicz

Himalayan leader

Rutkiewicz of Poland was the first European woman to climb Everest (1978) and the first woman to summit K2 (1986).

Women have shaped exploration, technical climbing, high-altitude expeditions and mountain culture throughout this history.

Today

A New Chapter in Mountain Exploration

Lessons from the past have shaped safer, more informed mountaineering. Every advance below traces back to a problem earlier climbers faced.

  • Technical clothing and improved equipment
  • More reliable weather forecasting
  • Digital navigation and satellite communication
  • Expedition medicine and acclimatization knowledge
  • Professional guiding standards
  • Environmental conservation
  • Responsible tourism and respect for local communities

Reflection

The Past Teaches Us How to Climb Tomorrow

Mountaineering history shows that summits are reached through preparation, teamwork, and humility. Accidents like the Matterhorn tragedy remind us to respect risk, never to chase it. Protecting fragile environments and honoring the communities who live in the mountains are part of that responsibility.

The mountain does not ask to be conquered. It asks to be understood.
